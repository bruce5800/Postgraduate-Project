AN INTERACTIVE GUIDE · PAPER §7 (BUDGET–STAKES LAW) · THESIS §10.2

Testing advice has a price —
and weak advice can't pay it

Some online-matching algorithms are handed a prediction about the future, watch a short sample to check whether it looks trustworthy, and then commit. This page plays through what that check costs — including the plot twist where completing our own impossibility proof disproved it, and what replaced it: a sharp price tag. Drag the sliders; no background needed beyond “what's an average.”

THE WHOLE ARGUMENT IN THREE LINES
  1. Test-and-fallback algorithms peek at a small prefix of the input, then either trust the prediction for everything that remains, or ignore it entirely.
  2. The law: deciding costs a peek of about σ²/g² arrivals — the contested share of the input divided by the squared stakes. Below that budget no rule whatsoever works; at it, counting agreements-minus-contradictions already works.
  3. The consequence: on inputs where the no-advice baseline is already strong, the stakes are so small that the price exceeds the whole input — and those are exactly the inputs our benchmark measures. The advice's upside is smaller than the cost of finding out whether to trust it.

The game · what the algorithm is allowed to do

Watch k arrivals, then bet the night

Picture a taxi rank on New Year's Eve. Passengers arrive one by one, and each must be put into a suitable car immediately — no take-backs. Before the night starts, a tipster sells you a forecast of tonight's crowd. You're allowed to watch the first k passengers for free. Then you must choose, once and for all: run the whole night by the tip sheet, or throw it away and dispatch the way you always have.

That's the entire setup, formally: n requests arrive; the algorithm sees the first k (with k much smaller than n), applies any rule it likes to what it saw, and commits to one of two modes:

Follow: route every remaining request the way the prediction suggests or  Fall back: ignore the prediction, use the standard no-advice method (Ranking)

A good algorithm should be consistent — when the tip is accurate, it collects nearly all the profit the tip makes possible — and robust — when the tip is garbage, it loses nothing compared to never having had a tip. Chapter 6 of the thesis found experimentally that no tuning of the deployed tests achieves both. This page explains what the real constraint is. Not a tuning problem — a bill.

Building block · paper §7.3 (cell constants)

One tiny dilemma — the only moment advice ever matters

Two parking bays, a and b. A hatchback that fits either bay arrives first and must park now. Later, maybe a van shows up (fits only bay a), or a trailer (only bay b) — or nobody comes. If you parked the hatchback in the wrong bay, the special vehicle drives away unserved. The tip sheet tells you which one to expect.

The whole hard instance in the proof is just thousands of these dilemmas glued together. In each cell: the flexible request F commits first; then with probability θs comes specialist α (fits only a), with probability θ(1−s) specialist β (only b), otherwise nobody. The advice tells you the likely direction — which side to keep free.

Adding it up · paper §7.3 (the affine law)

An exact exchange rate: advice error → lost profit

Glue thousands of parking dilemmas into one long night. The tip sheet gets a fraction φ of them pointed the wrong way. How do the night's takings depend on φ? Not roughly — exactly, on a straight line.
E[profit when following] = ρperfect − ½·ℓ1(p,q) In words: start from the perfect-advice score, subtract exactly half the advice's total error. Verified numerically to three decimals (parameters θ=0.6, ε=0.3 from the thesis).

The twist · paper §7.4 (payoff identity) — the step that killed our first theorem

The payoff was visible all along

We first believed the story ended at a sampling wall: judging a forecast of thousands of passenger types from 200 sightings looked like distribution testing, which provably needs almost as many samples as there are types. The machinery was cited, the numerics agreed, one “routine” step remained — and writing out that step disproved the theorem. Here is the killer, as a game.

Score each arriving passenger with the tip sheet in hand: +1 if they're the kind the tip bet on (its parking choice just paid off), −1 if they're the kind it bet against, 0 if the tip didn't matter for them. A two-line computation shows the average of this score is exactly twice the profit rate of following. You never need to learn the crowd — you count agreements minus contradictions.

E[score] = 2 × (profit of following − no-advice profit) / OPT The payoff identity — exact for every cell profile, magnitudes matched or not; verified to three decimals. The stakes are a per-arrival observable. Distribution testing was never required.

That is the real wall: not “testing is impossible,” but “the stakes can't pay for the peek.”
The other half · paper §7.2 (Lemma 1, fully proved) + §7.5(i)

Two tipsters you can't tell apart

The counting rule sets the price ceiling; this gadget sets the floor — why nothing can beat counting. Build two possible worlds: in world G the tipster is honest (following wins you δ), in world Bd the tipster is subtly poisoned (following costs you Δ). A short Hellinger computation shows that below the budget k* their first-k arrivals look almost identical — any statistic you compute from your peek differs between the worlds by at most a sliver, γk. Now: you still have to pick Follow or Fall back.
(1−ηc) ≤ ηr + γk In words: (upside you give up) + (damage you let through) ≥ 1 − (how much the worlds differ on your peek). When γk ≈ 0, the two losses must sum to ≈ everything.

Try this. Drag the threshold anywhere — the sum of the two losses never dips below 1−γk. Then shrink γk toward zero (which happens whenever your peek is below the budget k* = σ²/g²): the curves merge and you're choosing which loss to eat, not whether. This holds for any rule computed from the peek — a threshold, a neural net, anything — which is what makes the law's lower half algorithm-independent.
Assembly · paper §7.5 (Theorem 1) + §7.6 (the corollary)

Bolting the four parts together

① The construction (Gadgets A + B; proved, numerically verified) — a family of instances with exact per-cell stakes, where the specialist share σ² equals exactly twice the baseline slack: the same number measures how much there is to win and how noisy the peek is.
↓ the payoff identity (Gadget C): the stakes are a per-arrival observable
② Counting agreements decides at k ≈ σ²/g² — the price ceiling. One line of Bernstein; no calibration constants; works for any cell profile, matched magnitudes or not.
↓ the Hellinger computation + the trade-off inequality (Gadget D; fully proved)
③ Below ≈ σ²/g², nothing decides — the price floor. Two worlds needing opposite decisions look the same (γk→0), so the two losses must sum to ≈ 1 under any rule.
↓ put ceiling and floor together
④ The budget–stakes law: k* = Θ̃(σ²/g²), sharp. Corollary: even with the whole night as your peek, no upside below √(2·slack/n) is safely capturable — about 0.003 at the benchmark's parameters, where every measured upside lives below 0.01.

The advice's upside is smaller than the price of finding out whether to trust it.

HONEST STATUS (mirrors paper §7.8 and docs/T1_WITNESS_GAP.md)
The trade-off inequality, the cell constants, the exchange-rate law, and the payoff identity are proved, and both halves of the law use only elementary tools (Bernstein; Hellinger); every quantitative claim on this page is verified by seeded scripts (verify_witness_gap.py, verify_budget_stakes_hetero.py, run_directional_test.py). Full disclosure: an earlier version of this page taught an impossibility theorem whose engine was the tolerant-distribution-testing lower bound. Completing its last step disproved it — the payoff identity means the required indistinguishable witness pair cannot exist — and the refutation became the law you just played with. Open ends, stated plainly: the law's two sides can separate when the stakes hide in a vanishing sliver of low-signal cells, and whether a non-decomposable instance family can push the budget above σ²/g² is unknown.

Where to read more: the law with proofs in paper §7; the one-page outlook in thesis §10.2; the counting rule evaluated head-to-head on the benchmark in paper §5.4 (its misjudgement falls with peek size exactly where the deployed thresholds' rises).
